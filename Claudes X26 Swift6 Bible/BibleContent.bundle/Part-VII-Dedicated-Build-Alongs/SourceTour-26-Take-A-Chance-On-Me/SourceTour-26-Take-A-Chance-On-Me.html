Source Tour 26: Take A Chance On Me

Status: LIVE on the App Store (v2.1). Production multi-die roller for iPhone and iPad. The smallest-scale teaching app in this Source Tour set, intentionally so — it shows what a complete, polished, App-Store-shipping utility looks like at the simplest end of the size spectrum.

Two paths: install the live app to use the finished product, or clone the repo and read the source. The architecture is tiny — ~800 lines across seven Swift files — small enough to read in a single sitting and use as a template for your own first utility app. See Book 21 Git-And-GitHub for the clone workflow.

What Take A Chance On Me shows at production scale

  • What "minimum viable App Store app" actually looks like at the smallest defensible size — one feature done well plus the supporting surfaces (About, Send Feedback) that take a thin app from "demo" to "shippable utility."
  • Swift's Int.random(in:) for system randomness — the modern, type-safe replacement for arc4random_uniform and Int.random()'s C-bridged ancestors. Cryptographically-OK for game/utility use; not appropriate for security-sensitive randomness.
  • Configurable multi-die rolling — the user picks how many dice and what kind (d4, d6, d8, d10, d12, d20). Demonstrates how to take a single primitive operation (one random number) and parameterize it into a configurable feature.
  • A complete utility app at ~800 lines — the smallest member of this Source Tour set, intentionally selected to show the bottom of the size scale.

The dice-rolling primitive

The whole core of the app is one line, found at DiceBagView.swift:171:

let result = Int.random(in: 1...getMax(i))

Two pieces:

  • Int.random(in:) is Swift's standard system-RNG entry point. Pass a closed range; it returns a uniformly distributed integer in that range. The randomness comes from the system entropy pool (SystemRandomNumberGenerator under the hood). Good enough for dice, card shuffles, picking a random color, A/B-test bucketing — not appropriate for cryptographic purposes (use SecRandomCopyBytes for those).
  • getMax(i) is the configurability hook — a small lookup that returns 4 / 6 / 8 / 10 / 12 / 20 depending on which die kind the user picked for slot i. The variable is the upper bound, not the randomness itself.

That single line is the entire random-roll mechanic. Everything else in the app — the multi-slot UI, the animations, the result display, the haptic feedback, the dice-kind picker — is presentation around that one primitive.

Worth dwelling on because the lesson is general: most utility apps have a single small primitive at their core, and the rest of the codebase is presentation. Recognizing the primitive helps you keep it simple instead of reaching for libraries that do more than the app needs.

Why this is a "smallest defensible App Store app" reference

App Store Guideline 2.2 (Performance: Beta Testing) is what catches "you brought us a demo, not an app." The line between "thin utility" and "demo" is enforced subjectively by reviewers. Take A Chance On Me sits well above the line because it does these things, in addition to the dice roll itself:

  • Configurable inputs (multiple dice, choice of die size) — not a single-press gimmick
  • Polished output presentation — the result isn't just text, it's part of a designed UI
  • An About sheet that explains who built it and how to reach them
  • A Send Feedback channel that opens the user's mail composer
  • App icons across platform sizes, App Store screenshots, a privacy policy, a real description

The lesson: a one-feature utility can ship to the App Store if the supporting surfaces around the feature are real. The Signature trio from the book's distribution rule (MVP feature + Under the Hood view + About & Send Feedback) was designed exactly for this point of the size spectrum — small enough to keep simple, complete enough to ship.
